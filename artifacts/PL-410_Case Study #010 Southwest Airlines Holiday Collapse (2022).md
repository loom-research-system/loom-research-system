PL-401 — Case Study #001: Student Loan Forgiveness (2022–2023)
Period: 2022–2023
Domain: Education Policy / Executive Action
Analytical Role: External Constraint Failure

Central Research Question:
What happens when a policy has strong operational capacity but depends on a legal pathway that a single external actor can terminate?

Core Narrative:
The Biden administration announced broad student loan forgiveness under the HEROES Act. The Department of Education built the operational infrastructure to process millions of applications. The Supreme Court invalidated the legal basis in Biden v. Nebraska (2023). The policy died not because of implementation failure but because the entire pathway depended on a statutory interpretation that one external institution—the Court—could veto.

Primary Concepts to Test:

Veto Fragility (primary)

Governance Capacity (operational strength vs. legal vulnerability)

Dependency Chains (legal pathway as critical dependency)

Key Finding:
A system can be operationally excellent and still fail completely when its architecture depends on a single veto point it does not control.

PL-402 — Case Study #002: Healthcare.gov Launch (2013)
Period: October 2013
Domain: Health IT / Federal Procurement
Analytical Role: Execution Failure

Central Research Question:
What happens when a legally authorized, politically supported policy fails because the implementing institution lacks the specific capacities required to deliver it?

Core Narrative:
The Affordable Care Act mandated a federal health insurance marketplace. Healthcare.gov launched October 1, 2013, and immediately collapsed. The site could not handle even a fraction of projected traffic. Six people enrolled on day one. The failure was not legal or political—the law was upheld, the funding existed, the political will was present. The failure was operational: the contractors, procurement processes, and integration testing were inadequate for the task.

Primary Concepts to Test:

Execution Fragility (primary)

Implementation Debt (pre-existing procurement and technical debt)

Governance Capacity (domain-specific technical capacity)

Key Finding:
Capacity is not a single variable. Weakness in one domain—technical integration—can cause system-wide collapse despite strength in others.

PL-404 — Case Study #004: Acid Rain Program (1990–2010)
Period: 1990–2010
Domain: Environmental Policy / Market-Based Regulation
Analytical Role: Durable Success

Central Research Question:
What conditions produce durable governance success, and how does accumulated institutional capacity shape outcomes?

Core Narrative:
The Acid Rain Program, created under the 1990 Clean Air Act Amendments, established a cap-and-trade system for sulfur dioxide emissions. The program exceeded its emissions reduction targets ahead of schedule and at lower cost than projected. Success resulted from a combination of factors: two decades of accumulated institutional knowledge about air pollution regulation, a well-designed market mechanism, clear authority, and low implementation debt.

Primary Concepts to Test:

Institutional Maturation (primary)

Mechanism-Purpose Alignment (primary)

Accumulated Capacity (primary)

Key Finding:
Institutions that build capacity gradually over time—through repeated policy cycles, regulatory experience, and scientific knowledge—are better positioned to implement complex interventions successfully.

PL-405 — Case Study #005: Operation Warp Speed (2020–2021)
Period: 2020–2021
Domain: Public Health / Crisis Response
Analytical Role: Crisis Success

Central Research Question:
How can a temporary governance structure intentionally assembled for a specific mission bypass inherited implementation debt and achieve rapid results?

Core Narrative:
Operation Warp Speed was a public-private partnership created in May 2020 to accelerate COVID-19 vaccine development, manufacturing, and distribution. It compressed a process that typically takes years into months. Key design features included: parallel processing instead of sequential dependencies, government absorption of financial risk, funding for multiple vaccine candidates simultaneously, and a temporary organizational structure that bypassed normal bureaucratic constraints.

Primary Concepts to Test:

Assembled Capacity (primary)

Implementation Debt (bypassing inherited debt)

Mechanism-Purpose Alignment (temporary structure matched to mission)

Key Finding:
Capacity can be intentionally created through organizational design. Temporary structures can bypass inherited institutional limitations when given clear missions, adequate resources, and political backing.

PL-406 — Case Study #006: Flint Water Crisis (2014–2016)
Period: 2014–2016
Domain: Public Health / Environmental Justice
Analytical Role: Self-Correction Failure

Central Research Question:
How can a governance system continue to function procedurally while becoming epistemically incapable of recognizing its own failure?

Core Narrative:
Under emergency management, Flint switched its water source to the Flint River in 2014 to save money. Corrosion control chemicals were omitted. Lead leached from aging pipes. Residents complained immediately—rashes, hair loss, illness. The state denied problems, suppressed test results, and dismissed scientific evidence. Children were poisoned for over a year before the state acknowledged the crisis. The information existed. The system could not act on it.

Primary Concepts to Test:

Self-Correction Fragility (primary)

Governance Epistemology (primary)

Information Flow (primary)

Environmental Justice Debt (secondary)

Design Fragility (secondary)

Key Finding:
Governance systems can fail not because information is absent but because institutions cannot convert information into action. Self-correction requires more than knowledge—it requires mechanisms capable of acting on knowledge.

PL-407 — Case Study #007: Tobacco Master Settlement Agreement (1998–Present)
Period: 1998–Present
Domain: Public Health / Legal Settlement
Analytical Role: Mixed Outcome / Divergent Implementation

Central Research Question:
What happens when a governance mechanism has strong enforceable architecture for one objective and weak aspirational architecture for another?

Core Narrative:
The Tobacco Master Settlement Agreement (MSA) resolved litigation between 46 states and the tobacco industry. The financial architecture—binding payment mechanisms requiring the industry to pay over $200 billion—was durable and successful. The public health architecture—requiring states to use funds for smoking prevention—was aspirational and non-binding. States diverted most funds to general budgets. The settlement succeeded financially and failed health-wise simultaneously.

Primary Concepts to Test:

Mechanism-Purpose Alignment (primary)

Design Fragility (primary)

Objective Layer Separation (secondary)

Key Finding:
Implementation success in one domain does not guarantee policy success in another. The relationship between enforceable architecture and stated objectives determines which promises are kept and which become aspirational.

PL-408 — Case Study #008: California High-Speed Rail (2008–Present)
Period: 2008–Present
Domain: Infrastructure / Transportation
Analytical Role: Chronic Partial Implementation

Central Research Question:
What happens when an implementation timeline exceeds the stability of the political, financial, and institutional conditions required for completion?

Core Narrative:
California voters approved $10 billion in bonds for high-speed rail in 2008. The project promised to connect Los Angeles and San Francisco by 2029. Sixteen years later, costs have ballooned to over $100 billion, only a small segment is under construction, and completion is uncertain. The project persists—it has not been formally cancelled—but it cannot achieve its original objective within expected parameters. It consumes resources, produces partial progress, and continues indefinitely.

Primary Concepts to Test:

Temporal Fragility (primary)

Sustaining Capacity (primary)

Chronic Partial Implementation (primary)

Commitment Gap Debt (secondary)

Key Finding:
Long-horizon projects are vulnerable to the erosion of enabling conditions. A system can continue operating while producing sustained incompletion—not success, not failure, but perpetual partial progress.

PL-409 — Case Study #009: United States Digital Service (2014–Present)
Period: 2014–Present
Domain: Government Technology / Institutional Reform
Analytical Role: Self-Correction Success

Central Research Question:
Can governance systems convert failure recognition into durable corrective capacity through institutional design?

Core Narrative:
After the Healthcare.gov failure, the federal government diagnosed its technical capacity deficit and created the United States Digital Service—a permanent team of technical experts housed within OMB with White House backing and special hiring authorities. USDS has intervened in dozens of failing projects, improved digital service delivery, and survived three administrations. It represents successful self-correction capacity formation.

Primary Concepts to Test:

Self-Correction Fragility (primary — mutable property)

Assembled Capacity (primary — permanent vs. temporary)

Governance Epistemology (secondary)

Institutional Maturation (secondary)

Key Finding:
Self-correction fragility is not permanent. Visible failure, accurate diagnosis, and intentional institutional design can build durable corrective capacity. The critical variable is the mechanism that converts knowledge into action.

PL-410 — Case Study #010: Southwest Airlines Holiday Collapse (2022)
Period: December 2022
Domain: Private Sector / Operations Management
Analytical Role: Coordination Failure (Planned)

Central Research Question:
What happens when operational complexity grows faster than institutional coordination capacity?

Core Narrative (Planned):
Southwest Airlines experienced a catastrophic operational collapse during the 2022 holiday travel season. Legacy scheduling systems, crew tracking failures, and manual workarounds created a coordination breakdown that stranded over two million passengers. The airline had the planes, crews, and information—it could not coordinate them. The failure was not a lack of resources but a collapse of the systems that connected resources.

Primary Concepts to Test:

Implementation Debt (technical debt, coordination debt)

Governance Capacity (coordination capacity)

Dependency Chains (interconnected scheduling systems)

Information Flow (data existed, coordination failed)

Key Finding (Anticipated):
Private sector systems can fail for the same structural reasons as public sector systems. Loom's concepts should transfer across domains if they are identifying governance mechanisms rather than domain-specific phenomena.
