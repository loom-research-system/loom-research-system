CASE 006 · WATER GOVERNANCE

How did a change in water source become a prolonged public-health crisis?

The Flint water crisis is examined here as a governance and implementation problem: what was decided, what institutions had to do, what warnings emerged, and where the system failed to correct course.

RESEARCH ARTIFACTFlint Water Crisis: Corrosion Control FailureRepository status: PASSED

01 · WHAT WAS SUPPOSED TO HAPPEN?

Changing the water source required the system to manage corrosion.

The research artifact identifies the absence of corrosion-control treatment at the outset of the April 2014 Flint River switch as a core claim. That matters because water chemistry and infrastructure interact: a change in source water can alter how lead-bearing materials behave.

Loom's question is not simply whether a decision was made. It is whether the institutions responsible for implementing that decision had the authority, information, capacity, and feedback needed to make it safe.

A policy can be formally correct and still fail in implementation.

02 · WHAT HAPPENED INSTEAD?

Technical warnings and institutional assurances diverged.

The internal case record places three sources in conversation: the Michigan Water Advisory Task Force report, an EPA Region 5 draft review, and a 2015 Michigan Department of Environmental Quality public statement that described Flint water as safe.

The point of including contradictory evidence is deliberate. Loom does not define evidence as only the material that supports the preferred explanation.

03 · WHERE DID THE SYSTEM DIVERGE?

The research diagnostic is self-correction fragility.

The current Loom artifact characterizes the crisis as a failure of self-correction in which institutional bias overrode technical warnings. That is a research diagnosis, not a claim that one person or one decision alone caused the crisis.

THE LOOM QUESTION

Who had to act next?

Every outcome depends on a chain of actors, authorities, resources, information, and timing.

EVIDENCE & LIMITS

What Loom can say — and what it should not pretend to know.

Supported in the current artifact

Still being hardened
